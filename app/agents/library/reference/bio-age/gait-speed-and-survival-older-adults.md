Gait speed, meaning usual walking speed measured over a short course in meters per second (m/s), is a widely used physical performance marker of aging.

Studenski and colleagues (JAMA, 2011) pooled individual data from 9 cohort studies covering 34,485 community-dwelling adults aged 65 and older, followed for 6 to 21 years. Average age was 73.5 and average gait speed was 0.92 m/s. Findings:

- Faster walking speed was linked to longer survival in every study, with about 12% lower risk of death for each 0.1 m/s faster (pooled hazard ratio 0.88).
- Survival rose steadily across the whole range of speeds rather than at one cut-off.
- At age 75, predicted 10-year survival ranged from 19% to 87% in men and from 35% to 91% in women, from slowest to fastest walkers.
- A prediction using only age, sex and gait speed was as accurate as one using age, sex, chronic conditions, smoking, blood pressure, BMI and hospitalizations.

The study shows association in older adults; it does not prove that walking faster on purpose extends life, so evidence is labelled Moderate. The findings apply to people 65 and older and are not part of the PhenoAge formula. Keywords: walking speed, gait speed, how fast I walk and lifespan, mobility and aging.
