An umbrella review by Poole and colleagues (The BMJ, 2017) pulled together 201 meta-analyses of observational studies and 17 of interventional studies on coffee. Coffee drinking was more often linked with benefit than harm. The largest relative risk reductions appeared at about 3 to 4 cups a day compared with none, including roughly 17% lower all-cause mortality and 19% lower cardiovascular mortality. Higher intake was also linked with about 18% lower overall cancer incidence and lower rates of several liver, metabolic and neurological conditions.

Two exceptions stood out. In pregnancy, higher coffee intake was associated with low birth weight, preterm birth and pregnancy loss, and ACOG has a reaffirmed committee opinion on keeping caffeine moderate during pregnancy. Coffee was also associated with a higher fracture risk in women. The authors stressed that these are observational findings and randomized trials are needed to show cause, which is why this passage is graded Moderate.

The Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025-2030 do not set a coffee recommendation for adults. They advise choosing water and unsweetened beverages, avoiding energy drinks and sugar-sweetened drinks, and keeping caffeinated beverages away from children. Cream, sugar and syrups change the picture; added sugar counts against the guidelines' limits.
