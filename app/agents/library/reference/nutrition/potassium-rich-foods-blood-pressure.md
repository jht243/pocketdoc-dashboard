The National Academies 2019 report on sodium and potassium set adequate intakes (AIs) for potassium from the overall diet: 3,400 milligrams a day for adult men and 2,600 milligrams a day for adult women. These values are based on typical intakes of healthy adults with normal blood pressure and apply to the apparently healthy population. The report found moderate-strength evidence that more potassium lowers systolic and diastolic blood pressure, with larger effects in people with hypertension, but it could not define an intake that reduces chronic disease risk, so no potassium chronic disease target was set.

Most potassium in a healthy diet comes from food: vegetables, fruit, beans and lentils, potatoes, dairy and yogurt, nuts and fish. Diets built on these foods, such as DASH, pair higher potassium with lower sodium, which is the combination tied to lower blood pressure.

Caution applies outside the healthy population. The National Academies advise care with high supplemental potassium intake for people with or at high risk of reduced kidney function. Kidney disease and some blood pressure and heart medicines can raise blood potassium, so potassium supplements and potassium-based salt substitutes are a question for the clinician.

Members ask about potassium, bananas, high blood pressure foods, salt substitutes, or a potassium result on a blood test. Blood potassium on a lab report reflects kidney and hormone regulation more than diet.
