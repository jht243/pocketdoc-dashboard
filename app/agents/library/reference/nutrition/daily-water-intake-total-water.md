The National Academies (then the Institute of Medicine) set adequate intakes for water in 2004. Adequately hydrated women average about 2.7 liters (91 ounces) of total water a day, and men about 3.7 liters (125 ounces). Total water means water from all sources: plain water, other beverages including coffee, tea and milk, and food. About 80% typically comes from drinks and about 20% from food.

The panel found that most healthy people meet their daily needs by letting thirst guide them, so these figures are averages, not a quota to force. People who are very physically active or live in hot climates may need more, because prolonged activity and heat increase water losses. The panel deliberately gave no rule of thumb for how many glasses of water to drink, and concluded that caffeinated drinks count toward hydration as much as non-caffeinated ones.

The Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025-2030 call hydration a key factor in overall health and advise choosing water, still or sparkling, and unsweetened beverages. They also note that sodium and electrolytes are essential for hydration and that highly active people may need extra sodium to replace sweat losses.

Some conditions, such as heart failure and advanced kidney disease, can require fluid limits set by a clinician. Members ask about how much water to drink, hydration, dehydration, electrolytes, or urine color.
