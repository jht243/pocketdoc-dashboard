Once low testosterone is confirmed, guidelines recommend checking where the problem starts: in the testes, or in the pituitary gland that signals them.

The AUA testosterone deficiency guideline recommends:

- Measuring serum LH (luteinizing hormone) in men with low testosterone (Strong recommendation, Grade A). LH is the pituitary signal that tells the testes to make testosterone.
- Measuring serum prolactin when low testosterone is combined with a low or low-normal LH (Strong recommendation, Grade A).
- Evaluating for endocrine disorders, for example a pituitary problem, when prolactin stays high without a known cause (Strong recommendation).

The Endocrine Society 2018 guideline likewise recommends measuring LH and FSH (follicle stimulating hormone) to distinguish primary hypogonadism (testicular failure, typically with high LH and FSH) from secondary hypogonadism (pituitary or hypothalamic, with low or inappropriately normal LH and FSH).

The distinction matters because secondary causes can include treatable conditions such as high prolactin, pituitary tumors, opioid or steroid use, or obesity, and because it affects fertility planning. The AUA also lists conditions in which clinicians should consider testing testosterone even without symptoms, including unexplained anemia, bone loss, diabetes, chronic opioid or corticosteroid use, HIV, and male infertility. Interpretation is done by the treating clinician.
