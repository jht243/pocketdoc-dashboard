Testosterone stimulates red blood cell production, and a rise in hemoglobin and hematocrit (Hct, the percentage of blood made up of red cells) is the most frequent side effect of testosterone therapy (TRT). Very thick blood (erythrocytosis, polycythemia) may raise clotting risk.

The AUA testosterone deficiency guideline recommends:

- Measuring hemoglobin and hematocrit before starting testosterone and informing men about the risk of polycythemia (Strong recommendation, Grade A).
- If baseline hematocrit exceeds 50%, considering withholding therapy until the cause is explained.
- On therapy, a hematocrit of 54% or higher warrants intervention, for example adjusting the regimen or referral to hematology.

The Endocrine Society 2018 guideline:

- Treats a baseline hematocrit above 48% (above 50% for men living at high altitude) as a relative contraindication to starting.
- Recommends checking hematocrit at baseline, 3 to 6 months after starting, then annually.
- Recommends stopping therapy if hematocrit exceeds 54% until it falls to a safe level, evaluating for low oxygen and sleep apnea, and restarting at a reduced dose.

Lab reports show hematocrit as a percentage. Any change to therapy in response to a high hematocrit is made by the prescribing clinician.
