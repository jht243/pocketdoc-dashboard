Vitamin A comes in two forms that behave very differently when taken in excess, according to the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements:

- Preformed vitamin A (retinol and retinyl esters such as retinyl palmitate) comes from animal foods such as liver and dairy, fortified foods, and many supplements. Because it is fat-soluble, the body stores excess, mainly in the liver.
- Provitamin A carotenoids, chiefly beta-carotene, come from orange and green plant foods and some supplements, and the body converts them to vitamin A as needed.

Too much preformed vitamin A (hypervitaminosis A) causes harm. Acute toxicity after very large amounts brings severe headache, blurred vision, nausea, dizziness, muscle aches and poor coordination. Chronic high intake can cause dry skin, painful muscles and joints, fatigue, depression and abnormal liver tests. Intake above the upper limit in pregnancy can cause birth defects, as can retinoid acne and psoriasis drugs such as isotretinoin. The NIH upper intake level for vitamin A applies only to the preformed form.

Beta-carotene is not known to cause birth defects. Its most common effect in excess is carotenodermia, a harmless yellow-orange skin tint that fades when intake falls. However, high-amount beta-carotene supplements raised lung cancer risk in smokers (see the separate passage on beta-carotene and lung cancer). Checking supplement labels for the share of vitamin A given as retinol versus beta-carotene helps a clinician judge total preformed intake.
