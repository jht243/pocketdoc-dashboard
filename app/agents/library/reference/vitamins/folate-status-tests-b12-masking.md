The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements folate fact sheet describes folate testing (folic acid, vitamin B9, serum folate, RBC folate):

- Serum folate above 3 ng/mL indicates adequacy, but it is sensitive to recent intake and may not reflect long-term status.
- Red blood cell (erythrocyte) folate above 140 ng/mL indicates adequate status and reflects longer-term intake.
- Homocysteine is a functional marker; the most commonly used cut-off for elevated homocysteine is 16 µmol/L, though 12 to 14 µmol/L is also used. It is not specific, because B12 deficiency and kidney dysfunction also raise it.

Folate deficiency causes megaloblastic anemia, with large red cells (high MCV) like B12 deficiency. Frank folate deficiency is rare in the United States and usually occurs alongside other deficiencies. Groups at higher risk include people with alcohol use disorder, people with malabsorptive disorders, women of childbearing age and pregnant women.

Folate and B12 interact: experts have long been concerned that high folic acid intake might mask B12 deficiency until its neurological damage becomes irreversible, and the ODS notes the focus has shifted to whether large amounts of folate might worsen the anemia and cognitive symptoms of B12 deficiency. Checking B12 alongside folate avoids missing it.
