Zinc and copper compete for absorption in the gut, so taking zinc for a long time can drain copper. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements zinc and copper fact sheets explain:

- High zinc intake from supplements over a period of weeks can inhibit copper absorption, reduce immune function and lower HDL ('good') cholesterol. It is unlikely that anyone gets this much zinc from food alone.
- Excessive zinc supplements, or heavy use of zinc-containing denture creams, can cause copper deficiency. Preventing this is one reason the National Academies set the upper intake level for zinc, which counts all sources.
- Copper deficiency is otherwise uncommon. Its effects include anemia, loss of skin pigment, high cholesterol, connective tissue problems, bone loss, nerve problems with loss of coordination (ataxia), and a higher risk of infection. People with celiac disease are also at higher risk.
- Short-term excess zinc causes nausea, vomiting, stomach upset, headaches, dizziness and loss of appetite.

Groups more likely to have low zinc include people with gastrointestinal disorders or bariatric surgery, vegetarians and vegans, pregnant and breastfeeding women, and people with alcohol use disorder. Zinc also binds quinolone and tetracycline antibiotics and penicillamine, and some diuretics increase urinary zinc loss.

Anyone taking zinc long term, for example for immunity, skin or eye health, who develops unexplained anemia, numbness or balance problems should mention the zinc to a clinician, who may check copper and ceruloplasmin.
