The omega-3 index (O3I, red blood cell omega-3, RBC EPA plus DHA) is a blood test sold directly to consumers and by some longevity clinics. It reports EPA plus DHA, the long-chain omega-3 fats from fatty fish, as a percentage of all fatty acids in red blood cell membranes, which reflects longer-term intake rather than the last meal.

The index was proposed in 2004 by Harris and von Schacky in Preventive Medicine as a possible risk factor for death from coronary heart disease. Pooling earlier studies, they reported that an index of 8% or higher was associated with the greatest heart protection and 4% or lower with the least. Those bands are where the common 8% target comes from.

This target is Emerging and not part of any major guideline:

- No US cardiology or lipid guideline uses the omega-3 index to assess risk or set treatment, and it is not among the risk-enhancing factors in the 2026 lipid guideline.
- The association comes from observational data. In randomized trials summarized by the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, such as VITAL, standard fish oil supplements did not significantly reduce heart attack, stroke or cardiovascular death in people without heart disease, and high-amount omega-3 products slightly raised atrial fibrillation risk.

A low index mainly signals low fish intake. The AHA 2021 heart-healthy eating guidance advises eating fish regularly. Decisions about fish oil supplements belong with a clinician, particularly for anyone with atrial fibrillation or on a blood thinner.
