Serum magnesium (blood magnesium level) is the most commonly ordered magnesium test, but the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements magnesium fact sheet explains why it is a weak guide to body stores. About 50% to 60% of the body's magnesium is in bone and most of the rest is inside cells; less than 1% is in serum, and the kidneys keep the serum level under tight control. As a result, serum magnesium has little correlation with total body magnesium or tissue levels, and a normal result does not rule out depletion.

The ODS gives a normal serum range of 0.75 to 0.95 mmol/L and defines hypomagnesemia as below 0.75 mmol/L. US labs usually report magnesium in mg/dL; 0.75 to 0.95 mmol/L converts to about 1.8 to 2.3 mg/dL.

Other approaches include red blood cell, saliva and urine magnesium, ionized magnesium, and a magnesium-loading test, but the ODS states no single method is considered satisfactory, and a full assessment combines lab tests with clinical evaluation. Symptomatic deficiency is uncommon in healthy people because the kidneys conserve magnesium; early signs include loss of appetite, nausea, fatigue and weakness, progressing to cramps, numbness and abnormal heart rhythms.
