Vitamin E (alpha-tocopherol, tocopherol) supplements have not delivered the protection once hoped for and carry specific risks, according to the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements:

- Prostate cancer: the SELECT trial enrolled 35,533 healthy men 50 and older to test vitamin E, selenium, both, or placebo. It was stopped in 2008 because the supplements did not prevent prostate cancer. With longer follow-up, published in JAMA in 2011, men who had taken vitamin E alone had a statistically significant 17% higher risk of prostate cancer than men on placebo.
- Bleeding: vitamin E can inhibit platelet clumping and oppose vitamin K-dependent clotting factors. Two trials found more hemorrhagic stroke with alpha-tocopherol, one in Finnish male smokers and one in US male physicians, many of whom also took aspirin. Taken with anticoagulant or antiplatelet drugs such as warfarin, large amounts of vitamin E can raise bleeding risk, especially with low vitamin K intake.
- Two meta-analyses linked high-amount vitamin E supplements to small increases in all-cause mortality.

No adverse effects have been found from vitamin E in food such as nuts, seeds and vegetable oils. The USPSTF (2022) recommends against vitamin E supplements to prevent cardiovascular disease or cancer (grade D). The NIH sets a tolerable upper intake level for supplemental vitamin E; anyone on a blood thinner should review vitamin E use with the prescriber.
