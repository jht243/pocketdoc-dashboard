Iodine is needed to make thyroid hormone (T4, T3), so both too little and too much can affect the thyroid and TSH. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements iodine fact sheet explains where it comes from:

- Good food sources: seaweed (kelp, nori, kombu, wakame), fish and other seafood, eggs, and dairy, although the iodine in milk varies with farming practices. Plant-based milks contain little. Most fruits and vegetables are poor sources.
- Iodized salt: US table salt has been iodized voluntarily since the 1920s. However, most salt in the US diet comes from processed foods, which almost always use noniodized salt, and noniodized sea salt provides virtually no iodine.
- Supplements: many multivitamins contain iodine, some but not all prenatal vitamins do, and iodine-only or kelp products can contain amounts above the NIH upper intake level.

Groups at higher risk of low iodine include people who do not use iodized salt, pregnant women, vegans and people who eat little or no dairy, seafood or eggs. ODS describes iodine status using median urinary iodine concentration, which is a population measure; US surveys show intake in the general population has been stable since 2000, while many pregnant women run low.

Excess iodine can cause some of the same problems as deficiency, including goiter, raised TSH and hypothyroidism, and can also trigger hyperthyroidism or thyroiditis. People with autoimmune thyroid disease, such as Hashimoto thyroiditis, may react to amounts that are safe for others, so high-iodine supplements are worth reviewing with a clinician.
