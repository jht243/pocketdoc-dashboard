---
title: Calcium from food versus supplements and kidney stone risk
source: NIH ODS Calcium Fact Sheet updated 2026; Jackson et al. 2006 Women's Health Initiative, NEJM; Curhan et al. 1993, NEJM
evidence: Moderate
approved_by: pending
as_of: 2026-06

Calcium from food and calcium from supplements are not equivalent when it comes to kidney stones (renal calculi, calcium oxalate stones).

- Supplements: in the Women's Health Initiative trial of 36,282 postmenopausal women published in NEJM in 2006, those randomized to calcium plus vitamin D supplements had a 17% higher risk of kidney stones than placebo (hazard ratio 1.17). The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements calcium fact sheet likewise states that higher intakes of supplemental calcium might increase kidney stone risk.
- Food: in a prospective NEJM study of 45,619 men, those with the highest dietary calcium intake had a lower risk of symptomatic kidney stones than those with the lowest, a relative risk of 0.66 after adjustment.

The ODS also notes that some research suggests calcium supplements could raise cardiovascular risk while other studies find no link, and that more research is needed. Its general advice is that nutrient needs should be met primarily through diet, including fortified foods, with supplements used where diet falls short.

Together these data suggest food calcium and supplement calcium should be considered separately. For someone with a history of kidney stones, both dietary calcium and any calcium supplement are worth reviewing with a clinician rather than simply cutting calcium. The NIH sets a tolerable upper intake level for calcium covering food and supplements combined.
