On most lab reports LDL cholesterol (LDL-C, bad cholesterol) is calculated rather than measured directly. The lab measures total cholesterol, HDL and triglycerides, then estimates LDL-C with an equation:

- Friedewald equation (1972): LDL-C equals total cholesterol minus HDL minus triglycerides divided by 5. It works reasonably well when LDL-C is above about 100 mg/dL and triglycerides are under 150 mg/dL, but tends to underestimate LDL-C when LDL is low or triglycerides are 150 to 400 mg/dL.
- Martin-Hopkins equation (2013): replaces the fixed divisor of 5 with a factor that varies with the person's triglyceride and non-HDL levels.
- Sampson-NIH equation (2020): a newer formula developed at the NIH that performs well with triglycerides up to about 800 mg/dL.

The National Lipid Association 2021 statement prefers Martin-Hopkins, especially for LDL-C under 100 mg/dL with triglycerides of 150 to 400 mg/dL, and advises against using any estimating equation when triglycerides are 400 mg/dL or higher. Laboratory-medicine summaries of the 2026 ACC/AHA multisociety guideline report that it prefers Martin-Hopkins or Sampson-NIH over Friedewald. This matters most for people near an LDL goal, where Friedewald can make LDL look lower than it is. Keywords: calculated LDL, direct LDL, LDL formula, why my LDL differs between labs.
