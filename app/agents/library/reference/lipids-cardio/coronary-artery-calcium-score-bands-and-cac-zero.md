A coronary artery calcium (CAC) score, also called a calcium score or heart scan, comes from a noncontrast CT of the heart arteries and is reported in Agatston units, often with a percentile for age and sex. Clinical summaries of the 2026 ACC/AHA multisociety dyslipidemia guideline describe these bands for adults with borderline or intermediate risk:

- CAC 0: no detectable calcified plaque; cholesterol-lowering therapy may be deferred, with risk reassessed in about 5 years.
- CAC 1 to 99: favors starting therapy, with an LDL-C goal under 100 mg/dL.
- CAC 100 or higher, or at or above the 75th percentile: treated as subclinical atherosclerosis, with an LDL-C goal under 70 mg/dL.
- CAC 1,000 or higher: treated as very high risk, with an LDL-C goal under 55 mg/dL.

In the MESA study (Nasir et al., JACC 2015), 44% of adults eligible for statins under the older guideline had a CAC of 0, and their event rate was low, about 4.2 per 1,000 person-years. The earlier 2018 guideline noted that a zero score is less reassuring in people who smoke, have diabetes, or have a family history of premature heart disease. Summaries also note that repeat scans are not used to track treatment, because calcium can increase even when LDL is well controlled.
