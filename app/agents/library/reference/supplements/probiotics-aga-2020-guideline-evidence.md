Probiotics are live microorganisms, mostly Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium and the yeast Saccharomyces boulardii, sold as supplements and in foods such as yogurt and kefir. Most are dietary supplements that do not need FDA approval, and effects depend on the specific strain: a benefit seen with one strain does not carry over to another.

The American Gastroenterological Association's 2020 guideline (Su et al.) found the evidence too weak for most digestive conditions:
- Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis and treatment of C. difficile infection: probiotics only in the context of a clinical trial (knowledge gap).
- Children with acute infectious diarrhea (gastroenteritis): suggests against probiotics.
- Preventing C. difficile infection in adults and children taking antibiotics: suggests certain specific strains or combinations, including S. boulardii (conditional recommendation, low-quality evidence). AGA notes people who weigh cost or possible harms more heavily could reasonably choose none.
- Pouchitis and preterm, low-birth-weight infants: specific combinations are suggested.

The NIH National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) adds that probiotics appear safe for most healthy people but carry higher risk for people with serious illness or weakened immune systems. In 2023 the FDA warned that probiotics given to premature infants can cause severe, potentially fatal infections. Some products have contained microbes not listed on the label.

Anyone with a weakened immune system, a central line or a serious illness should check with their clinician before taking probiotics.
