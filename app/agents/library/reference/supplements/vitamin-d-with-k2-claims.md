Vitamin D3 plus K2 combination products are marketed with the claim that K2 sends calcium into bones and keeps it out of arteries. The idea has a biological basis but limited trial support, so it is graded Emerging.

The biology: vitamin K comes as K1 (phylloquinone, mainly from leafy greens) and K2 (menaquinones such as MK-4 and MK-7, from fermented foods like natto and cheese, and gut bacteria). Vitamin K activates proteins including osteocalcin in bone and matrix Gla protein (MGP), which may help prevent calcification of blood vessels.

What the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements (ODS) fact sheet says:
- Bones: it is unclear whether any form of vitamin K supplement reduces osteoporosis risk. Older, mostly Japanese trials of MK-4 reported fewer fractures, but later trials found no effect on bone density, and the role of vitamin D given alongside complicates comparisons.
- Arteries and heart: observational studies link higher K2 intake with less arterial calcification, but few trials exist, and the role of vitamin K in arterial calcification and heart disease remains unclear and under study.

There is no tolerable upper intake level for vitamin K, because no adverse effects have been reported from food or supplements. The key exception is warfarin (Coumadin): vitamin K, including K2 in a D3/K2 product, counteracts it, and people taking warfarin need to keep vitamin K intake consistent. Starting a D3/K2 product while on warfarin should be cleared with the prescriber first.
