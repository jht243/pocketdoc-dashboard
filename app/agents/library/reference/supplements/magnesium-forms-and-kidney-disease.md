Magnesium supplements come in many forms: magnesium oxide, citrate, glycinate, chloride, lactate, aspartate, sulfate, malate and others. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements (ODS, updated January 2026) says forms that dissolve well in liquid tend to be absorbed better, and that aspartate, citrate, lactate and chloride are generally more bioavailable than magnesium oxide and magnesium sulfate. Magnesium is also an ingredient in some laxatives and antacids.

The kidneys control the body's magnesium balance and remove the excess. That is why magnesium from food carries little risk for healthy people, while large amounts from supplements or medicines can cause diarrhea, nausea and cramping, and very large amounts can cause magnesium toxicity: low blood pressure, vomiting, trouble breathing, irregular heartbeat and, rarely, cardiac arrest. The Institute of Medicine noted that the risk of too much magnesium (hypermagnesemia) rises when kidney function is impaired, and people with kidney disease were excluded from the group its upper limit was built for.

The NIH sets a tolerable upper intake level for magnesium from supplements and medicines (not from food); the prescriber or clinician sets any amount. Magnesium can also interact with oral bisphosphonates (osteoporosis drugs) and tetracycline and quinolone antibiotics, and diuretics and PPIs can lower magnesium levels.

Anyone with chronic kidney disease (CKD, a reduced eGFR) should check with their clinician before taking magnesium supplements, laxatives or antacids.
