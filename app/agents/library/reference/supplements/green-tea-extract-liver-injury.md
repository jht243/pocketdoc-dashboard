Green tea extract (GTE), concentrated catechins such as EGCG (epigallocatechin gallate), is found in more than 100 supplements, especially weight-loss and "fat burner" products. Drinking green tea as a beverage has not been linked to liver injury.

The NIH LiverTox database (updated November 2020) rates green tea extract likelihood score A, a well-established cause of clinically apparent liver injury. Key points:
- More than 100 cases have been reported. Injury usually appears 1 to 6 months after starting, typically as an acute hepatitis-like illness with very high ALT and AST, sometimes with jaundice. Fatal acute liver failure and liver transplants have occurred.
- In a large trial of green tea extract in postmenopausal women, ALT rose in 6.7% of those taking it versus 0.7% on placebo.
- The injury appears idiosyncratic and immune-related and is linked to the HLA-B*35:01 gene variant, the same variant tied to turmeric liver injury.
- Most people recover within one to two months of stopping.

A 2020 US Pharmacopeia (USP) review found that taking extract on an empty stomach greatly increases catechin absorption. USP's green tea extract monograph therefore carries cautionary label wording: do not take on an empty stomach, take with food, avoid with liver problems, and stop and seek care if liver symptoms appear.

Fatigue, nausea, dark urine, itching or yellowing of the skin or eyes while taking a green tea extract product warrants prompt contact with a clinician, and a liver panel is a reasonable test to raise.
