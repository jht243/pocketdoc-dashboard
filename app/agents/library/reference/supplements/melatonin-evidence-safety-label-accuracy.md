Melatonin is a hormone the brain makes at night. In the US it is sold as a dietary supplement, while several other countries regulate it as a prescription drug.

Evidence, per the NIH National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH, updated May 2024):
- Chronic insomnia: the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (2017) and the American College of Physicians (2016) found the evidence too weak to recommend melatonin; CBT-I is the recommended first treatment (see the sleep library).
- Jet lag: small studies suggest it may reduce symptoms after eastward or westward flights.
- Shift work: studies were small, low quality or inconclusive.
- People with dementia: the AASM (2015) recommends against it.

Safety: short-term use appears safe for most adults, with mild side effects such as headache, dizziness, nausea and sleepiness; long-term safety is not well studied. NCCIH advises medical supervision for people with epilepsy or taking blood thinners, and notes a lack of safety data in pregnancy.

Label accuracy: a 2023 JAMA study of 25 melatonin gummy products found 22 were mislabeled; one contained no melatonin and the rest held 74% to 347% of the labeled amount. Children's accidental ingestions have risen sharply; NCCIH cites poison control reports in people 19 and under climbing from 8,337 in 2012 to 52,563 in 2021. Choosing a third-party-tested product (see the USP and NSF seals passage) and storing melatonin out of children's reach are practical steps.

Long-term nightly use is worth discussing with a clinician.
