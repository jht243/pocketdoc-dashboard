Calcium supplements, such as calcium carbonate (also found in many antacids) and calcium citrate, are widely taken for bone health and osteoporosis prevention. A common question is whether they harm the heart.

The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements (ODS, updated June 2026) summarizes the evidence as mixed: observational studies link low calcium intake with higher risk of high blood pressure, stroke and atherosclerosis, some cohort studies and trials have suggested calcium supplements might raise cardiovascular risk, and other studies have found no link. ODS says more research is needed.

In 2016 the National Osteoporosis Foundation (NOF, now the Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation) and the American Society for Preventive Cardiology (ASPC) reviewed the evidence. They concluded, based on moderate-quality evidence, that calcium with or without vitamin D, from food or supplements, has no relationship, beneficial or harmful, with heart disease, stroke, cardiovascular death or overall death in generally healthy adults. They added that total calcium intake from food and supplements that stays within the tolerable upper intake level set by the National Academy of Medicine should be considered safe from a cardiovascular standpoint.

Because studies still disagree, this passage is graded Moderate. ODS advises meeting calcium needs mainly through food, with supplements filling gaps. Kidney stone risk is covered in the passage on calcium from food versus supplements. People with heart disease, kidney disease or a history of kidney stones should review calcium supplements with a clinician, who sets any amount.
