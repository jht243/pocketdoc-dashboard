Creatine (usually creatine monohydrate) is, in the words of the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, one of the most thoroughly studied and widely used supplements for exercise performance. It helps most with repeated short bursts of high-intensity effort such as sprinting and weight lifting, and little for endurance sports. The ODS fact sheet (updated 2024) says creatine is considered safe for short-term use by healthy adults; the most consistent effect is weight gain from water retention.

Kidneys: the International Society of Sports Nutrition 2017 position stand reviewed studies including a 21-month study in college football players and concluded there is no compelling evidence that creatine harms kidney function in healthy or clinical populations. It still advises people with kidney disease to consult their physician first, and the KDIGO 2024 kidney guideline notes rare case reports of kidney inflammation (interstitial nephritis) linked to creatine.

Lab tests: the body converts creatine to creatinine, which the kidneys excrete. The KDIGO 2024 CKD guideline lists high-protein diets and creatine supplements among non-kidney factors that can raise serum creatinine, which can make a creatinine-based eGFR look lower than true kidney function. In that situation KDIGO points to cystatin C, a kidney marker not affected by muscle or creatine, as a useful check.

Anyone taking creatine whose creatinine is high or eGFR low should tell the clinician about the supplement before the result is interpreted.
