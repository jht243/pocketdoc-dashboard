A Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) is the highest daily intake of a nutrient that is unlikely to cause harm in almost all healthy people. The National Academies' Food and Nutrition Board sets ULs as part of the Dietary Reference Intakes. Going above the UL raises the chance of side effects; it is a safety ceiling, not a target. The NIH sets these limits; the prescriber or clinician sets any amount above the usual recommended intake.

The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements groups nutrients this way:
- ULs that count all sources (food, drinks, fortified foods and supplements): calcium, choline, copper, fluoride, iodine, iron, manganese, molybdenum, nickel, phosphorus, selenium, vitamin B6, vitamin C, vitamin D and zinc. Examples of harm: very high vitamin B6 can cause severe nerve damage, and too much iron can be fatal.
- ULs that apply only to supplements and fortified foods: folate (as folic acid), magnesium, niacin and vitamin E.
- A UL for one form only: vitamin A, where preformed vitamin A (retinol, retinyl palmitate) has a UL because excess can cause liver damage and birth defects, while beta-carotene does not.
- No UL set: biotin, chromium, pantothenic acid, riboflavin, thiamin, vitamin B12 and vitamin K. ODS adds there is still no reason to take more than recommended amounts.

Stacking a multivitamin with single-nutrient products is the most common way people pass a UL without realizing it, so the full supplement list is worth reviewing with a clinician.
