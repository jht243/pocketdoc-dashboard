Berberine is a plant compound found in goldenseal, barberry, Oregon grape and other plants. Social media sometimes calls it "nature's Ozempic", but it is not a GLP-1 medicine.

Evidence: the NIH National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH, 2023) says some studies suggest berberine might help with weight loss but the evidence is not conclusive: a 2022 review found decreases in weight and BMI, but many included studies had a high risk of bias, results were inconsistent, products varied widely, and most studies were done in Asia in people with diabetes or fatty liver disease. NCCIH also describes some evidence of a modest effect on blood glucose (A1c, fasting glucose) in type 2 diabetes, again from studies of variable quality. This is why the passage is graded Emerging.

Safety and interactions:
- Side effects are mainly digestive: nausea, abdominal pain, bloating, constipation or diarrhea.
- NCCIH notes berberine interacts with cyclosporine (a transplant drug). A human study found that repeated berberine use lowered the activity of the drug-metabolizing enzymes CYP2D6, CYP2C9 and CYP3A4, so it may raise levels of medicines cleared by those enzymes.
- Because it may lower glucose, combining it with diabetes medicines could add to their effect.
- It is likely unsafe for infants and may be unsafe in pregnancy or breastfeeding.

Anyone taking prescription medicines, especially for diabetes, should raise berberine with the prescriber or pharmacist first.
