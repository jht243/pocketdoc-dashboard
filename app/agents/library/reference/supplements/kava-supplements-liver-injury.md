Kava (kava kava, Piper methysticum) is a Pacific Island root used as a ceremonial drink and sold in concentrated supplements, teas and kava bars for anxiety, stress and sleep. Its active compounds, kavalactones, have relaxing, alcohol-like effects.

Liver injury: in March 2002 the FDA issued a consumer advisory that kava-containing dietary supplements may be associated with severe liver injury, citing more than 25 reports abroad of hepatitis, cirrhosis and liver failure and a US case of a previously healthy young woman who needed a liver transplant. Germany, Switzerland, France, Canada and the United Kingdom restricted or removed kava products. The FDA advised people with liver disease or taking medicines that affect the liver to talk with a physician before using kava.

The NIH LiverTox database (updated April 2018) rates kava likelihood score A, a well-known cause of clinically apparent liver injury, while noting that some cases were poorly documented and that adulterants may play a role. Between 50 and 100 cases have been described. Symptoms typically appear 2 to 24 weeks after starting: fatigue, nausea, very high liver enzymes (ALT, AST) and jaundice. Most cases resolve within 1 to 3 months of stopping, but some progress to liver failure, transplant or death, and restarting kava brings the injury back quickly.

Anyone using kava should tell their clinician, and dark urine, yellowing of the skin or eyes, or unusual fatigue warrants prompt medical attention.
