Dietary supplements are not approved by the FDA before they are sold. Under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA), manufacturers are responsible for making sure their products are safe and their labels truthful; FDA does not test supplements for safety or effectiveness before they reach shelves, and acts mainly after a problem appears (inspections, adverse event reports, warnings, recalls). Companies must follow FDA Good Manufacturing Practices (GMPs) for identity, purity, strength and composition. Labels may make structure or function claims ("supports heart health") but must carry a disclaimer that FDA has not evaluated the claim and that the product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent disease.

Third-party seals help with quality. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements (ODS) names three organizations that test products: U.S. Pharmacopeia (USP Verified), NSF International, and ConsumerLab.com. A seal indicates the product was properly manufactured, contains the ingredients listed on the label, and does not contain harmful levels of contaminants. ODS stresses that seals do not guarantee a product is safe or effective. NSF Certified for Sport additionally screens products for substances banned in sport, which matters for tested athletes.

Practical points members ask about: a seal does not make a high amount safe or a weak ingredient work; the FDA has repeatedly found hidden prescription drug ingredients in products marketed for weight loss, muscle building and sexual enhancement; and every supplement belongs on the medication list shared with clinicians.
