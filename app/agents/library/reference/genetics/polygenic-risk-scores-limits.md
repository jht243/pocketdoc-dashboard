A polygenic risk score adds up the small effects of hundreds to millions of common variants into one number for a condition, such as coronary artery disease, type 2 diabetes or breast cancer. Many consumer reports present results this way, often as "higher than average" or a percentile.

The American Heart Association's 2022 scientific statement found that coronary artery disease scores predict risk independently of traditional risk factors and that people in the top few percent can carry risk comparable to some single-gene conditions. It also found that adding a score to clinical risk calculators improves prediction only modestly, that scores developed mostly in people of European ancestry perform worse in other ancestries, and that evidence that acting on a score improves outcomes is still limited.

For a member, a polygenic score is context, not a verdict. The measured risk factors, such as LDL and ApoB, Lp(a), blood pressure, A1c and smoking status, carry most of the actionable information. A high score is a reasonable thing to mention to a clinician when deciding how closely to follow those numbers.
