Direct-to-consumer DNA tests such as 23andMe and AncestryDNA read several hundred thousand fixed positions on a genotyping chip. They are accurate for common variants but were not built to find rare ones, and they read only a few selected variants in genes where hundreds of harmful variants are known.

When people took raw consumer data to third-party interpretation sites, a 2018 study in Genetics in Medicine re-tested the reported variants in a clinical laboratory: about 40 percent of the variants were false positives, and some variants the sites called "increased risk" were classified as benign by clinical labs.

Two practical rules follow. A worrying result from a consumer test or a raw-data interpretation site should be confirmed with clinical-grade testing, ideally with a genetic counselor, before anyone acts on it. And a reassuring result does not rule a hereditary condition out, because the test did not look at most of the gene.

The National Society of Genetic Counselors supports consumer access to testing but advises discussing medically significant results with a genetics professional.
