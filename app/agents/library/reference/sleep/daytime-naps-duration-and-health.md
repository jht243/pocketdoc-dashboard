A dose-response meta-analysis by Yamada and colleagues (Sleep, 2015) pooled 11 prospective cohort studies with 151,588 adults followed for about 11 years on average.

- Napping for 60 minutes or more a day was associated with about 82% higher risk of cardiovascular disease and about 27% higher all-cause mortality compared with not napping.
- Naps shorter than 60 minutes were not significantly associated with either outcome.
- For cardiovascular disease the curve was J-shaped: risk dipped slightly with naps up to about 30 minutes, rose a little to about 45 minutes, then rose sharply with longer naps.

A later meta-analysis of 21 studies (PLOS ONE, 2024) found the same pattern: naps under an hour showed no significant link with mortality or cardiovascular disease, while naps of an hour or more did.

These studies are observational. Long daytime naps may be a sign of something else, such as short or poor nighttime sleep, sleep apnea, depression or another illness, rather than a cause of disease. No major guideline sets a nap rule for healthy adults, so this passage is graded Emerging. Frequent, unexplained daytime sleepiness despite enough nighttime sleep is worth raising with a clinician.
