Consumer sleep trackers such as rings, watches, wristbands and bedside devices estimate sleep from movement and heart rate, not from brain waves. Polysomnography (an in-lab sleep study) remains the reference standard.

In a laboratory study published in Sleep (Chinoy and colleagues, 2021), 34 healthy young adults wore several consumer devices during polysomnography over three nights. All devices detected sleep well (epoch-by-epoch sensitivity above 0.93) but were much weaker at detecting wake (specificity roughly 0.18 to 0.54), so they tend to count quiet, still wakefulness as sleep. Several performed as well as or better than research actigraphy for sleep and wake, accuracy dropped on nights of disturbed sleep, and agreement on sleep stages (light, deep, REM) was mixed.

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine 2018 position statement says consumer sleep technology cannot currently diagnose sleep disorders, that devices intended to diagnose or treat sleep disorders need FDA clearance, and that clinicians should treat device data as context within a full evaluation, not as a replacement for validated testing.

Practical reading: nightly deep sleep or REM minutes are rough estimates; trends in total sleep, timing and consistency over weeks are more useful than one night. Members ask about sleep score, deep sleep, REM, Oura, Whoop, Apple Watch or Fitbit accuracy.
