Light reaching the eyes sets the body clock (circadian rhythm), suppresses the sleep hormone melatonin and increases alertness. This effect runs through melanopsin, a light sensor in the retina most sensitive to blue-enriched light.

In 2022 an international group of sleep, circadian and lighting experts led by Brown published the first consensus recommendations for healthy indoor light exposure in PLOS Biology. Expressed in melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance (melanopic EDI), measured at the eye:
- Daytime: at least 250 lux, ideally from daylight. Brighter days were linked to better alertness, mood and sleep.
- Evening, starting at least 3 hours before bedtime: no more than 10 lux, using dimmer, warmer light where possible.
- During sleep: no more than 1 lux; the bedroom should be as dark as possible.

The panel noted that night-shift or blue-light-filter settings on phones and screens only modestly reduce the alerting light they give off, so they work best combined with dimming screens and keeping room lighting low. Bright daytime light may partly buffer the effects of unavoidable evening light.

These are expert consensus targets built on laboratory and real-world studies rather than outcome trials, so this passage is graded Moderate. The practical message is bright days, dim evenings and dark nights.

Members ask about screens before bed, blue light, blue-light glasses, phone use at night, morning sunlight, or a dark bedroom.
