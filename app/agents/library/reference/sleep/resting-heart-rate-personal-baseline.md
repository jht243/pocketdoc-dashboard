A Scripps Research study by Quer and colleagues (PLOS ONE, 2020) analyzed daily resting heart rate (RHR) from wrist-worn trackers in 92,457 adults across the US. Mean daily resting heart rate ranged from 40 to 109 beats per minute (bpm) between individuals, with an overall mean of 65 bpm, so one person's normal could differ from another's by nearly 70 bpm. Age, sex, body mass index and average sleep duration together explained less than 10% of that difference between people.

Within each person, resting heart rate was much steadier: individual values seldom moved by more than 10 bpm over the year. The authors suggested this makes a person's own baseline a meaningful reference, and that a sustained departure from it may be worth attention. The study had no information on participants' health, so it could not define what counts as a healthy value.

Practical reading: compare recent 7-day and 30-day averages with a member's own usual range rather than a population chart. Short-lived rises can follow things such as alcohol, illness, short sleep or hard training, so a single high night is not a finding. Resting heart rate climbing for weeks without an obvious reason belongs with a clinician. Using these trends for health decisions is still emerging.

Members ask about resting heart rate, RHR, pulse, heart rate going up, or a high or low resting heart rate on Oura, Whoop, Apple Watch or Fitbit.
