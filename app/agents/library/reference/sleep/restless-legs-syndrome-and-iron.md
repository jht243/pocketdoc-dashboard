Restless legs syndrome (RLS), also called Willis-Ekbom disease, is a strong, nearly irresistible urge to move the legs with uncomfortable sensations that worsens at rest, typically in the evening or at night, and is relieved by movement. It commonly disturbs sleep.

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine guideline (published January 2025) makes iron central to RLS care:
- Clinicians should regularly check iron studies, including ferritin and transferrin saturation, in everyone with clinically significant RLS, ideally on a morning draw with no iron for 24 hours before.
- Consensus thresholds suggest iron treatment for adults with RLS when ferritin is 75 ng/mL or lower or transferrin saturation is below 20%, with oral or intravenous iron, and with intravenous iron only (not oral) when ferritin is between 75 and 100 ng/mL, where oral iron is poorly absorbed. For children the ferritin threshold is below 50 ng/mL.
- The guideline states these RLS thresholds are higher than the iron-deficiency cut-offs used for the general population, and that they come from expert consensus not yet tested in trials (graded Moderate here).
- The first step in management is addressing aggravating factors: alcohol, caffeine, some antihistamine, serotonergic and dopamine-blocking medicines, and untreated sleep apnea.
- The guideline moved away from dopamine-agonist medicines such as pramipexole and ropinirole as standard treatment because of augmentation, a long-term worsening of symptoms.

The form and amount of any iron treatment are for the clinician. Members ask about restless legs, leg jerks at night, or low ferritin and sleep.
