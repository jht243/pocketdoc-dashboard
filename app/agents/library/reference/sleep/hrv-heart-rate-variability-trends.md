Heart rate variability (HRV) is the beat-to-beat variation in time between heartbeats, measured in milliseconds (ms). Most wearables report RMSSD, which the Shaffer and Ginsberg 2017 review describes as the main time-domain measure of vagally mediated, or parasympathetic, heart rate changes.

Key facts from that review:
- HRV values decline with age, and differ by sex.
- Reference values depend on how long the recording is; 24-hour, short-term (about 5 minutes) and ultra-short-term readings are not interchangeable. Overnight wearable readings use yet other windows, so numbers from different devices are not directly comparable.
- Low HRV is associated with poorer health and higher mortality in clinical populations, but higher is not always better: some heart rhythm problems, such as atrial fibrillation, can raise HRV readings.

Because HRV varies so widely between people and devices, there is no single universal normal value for wearable HRV. The more useful reading is a member's own trend: comparing recent 7-day and 30-day averages with their usual range. Day-to-day readings can shift with things such as alcohol, hard training, illness, short sleep or stress, which is why single nights are noisy. Using wearable HRV to guide daily decisions is still an emerging practice.

A sustained fall over weeks alongside symptoms is worth raising with a clinician. Members ask about HRV, readiness score, recovery score, or low HRV.
