evidence: Moderate
approved_by: pending

The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, 2nd edition (HHS, 2018) state that adults who do more physical activity have lower risk of developing cancers of the bladder, breast, colon, endometrium, esophagus (adenocarcinoma), kidney, lung and stomach. The effects appear to apply to men and women regardless of body weight.

The 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines Advisory Committee's review, summarized by McTiernan and colleagues in Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise (2019), drew on 45 reports covering hundreds of studies and several million people. It found strong evidence for seven of these cancers (all except lung), with roughly 10% to 20% lower relative risk in the most active compared with the least active people. The committee also found that more activity was associated with lower risk of dying from breast, colorectal and prostate cancer in people already diagnosed, with relative reductions in some analyses approaching 40% to 50%.

This evidence comes from observational cohort studies rather than randomized trials, so activity is linked with lower risk without proof of cause, and the passage is graded Moderate. Researchers still debate the exact amount of activity needed for each cancer.

The guideline activity targets apply here: 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week, plus muscle strengthening. Members ask about exercise and cancer prevention, staying active after cancer, or lifestyle and cancer risk. Screening tests remain the way to find cancer early.
