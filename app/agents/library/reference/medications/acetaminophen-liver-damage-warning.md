Acetaminophen (paracetamol, Tylenol) is a common pain and fever reliever. It is also an ingredient in many combination products: cold and flu medicines (such as NyQuil), PM sleep aids, and prescription opioid combinations such as hydrocodone-acetaminophen (Norco, Vicodin) and oxycodone-acetaminophen (Percocet).

The NIH LiverTox database says acetaminophen is harmless at low doses but directly toxic to the liver in overdose, and that it is the major cause of acute liver failure in the United States, Europe and Australia. Unintentional overdose often happens when several products containing acetaminophen are taken at once, or when high amounts are taken over several days. Fasting, malnutrition, heavy alcohol use and existing liver disease raise the risk. Even ordinary use can cause temporary, usually harmless rises in ALT on a liver panel.

US rules require every over-the-counter acetaminophen product to carry a liver warning: severe liver damage may occur with more than the maximum daily amount, with other drugs containing acetaminophen, or with 3 or more alcoholic drinks every day while using it. In 2011 the FDA limited the acetaminophen strength in prescription combination pills and added a boxed warning about severe liver injury.

The maximum daily amount is printed on each label and is set by the prescriber for prescription products. Suspected overdose is an emergency even without symptoms, because an antidote (acetylcysteine) works best when given early; Poison Control is 1-800-222-1222.
