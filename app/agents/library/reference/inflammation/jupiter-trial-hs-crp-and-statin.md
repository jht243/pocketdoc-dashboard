The JUPITER trial (Ridker et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 2008) tested whether a statin helps apparently healthy people whose cholesterol was not high but whose hs-CRP (high-sensitivity C-reactive protein) was raised. It enrolled 17,802 adults with LDL cholesterol under 130 mg/dL and hs-CRP of 2.0 mg/L or higher and randomly assigned them to rosuvastatin or placebo.

The trial was stopped early, after a median of 1.9 years, because of clear benefit:

- Rosuvastatin lowered LDL cholesterol by 50% and hs-CRP by 37%.
- The combined rate of heart attack, stroke, artery procedures, hospitalization for unstable angina or cardiovascular death fell by 44% (hazard ratio 0.56).
- Heart attack fell by about half (hazard ratio 0.46), stroke by about half (0.52), and death from any cause by 20% (0.80).
- There was no significant increase in muscle disease or cancer, but physician-reported diabetes was more common on the statin.

Because the statin lowered both LDL and hs-CRP, the trial could not separate the two effects. JUPITER underlies the use of hs-CRP as a risk marker; the 2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline lists an hs-CRP of 2 mg/L or higher, confirmed on repeat testing, as a risk-enhancing factor, matching the JUPITER entry threshold.
