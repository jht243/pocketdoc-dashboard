The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR, sed rate) measures how quickly red blood cells settle to the bottom of a tall, thin tube of blood. Inflammation raises proteins in the blood that make red cells clump together, and clumps sink faster, so a higher ESR suggests more inflammation.

MedlinePlus, from the NIH National Library of Medicine, stresses that the ESR is nonspecific: it shows that inflammation may be present but not where it is or what is causing it, and inflammation can also be present with a normal ESR. It is used alongside symptoms, history and other tests, and is often ordered together with C-reactive protein (CRP).

Conditions linked to a high ESR include giant cell arteritis, polymyalgia rheumatica, rheumatoid arthritis and other autoimmune diseases, vasculitis, infections, inflammatory bowel disease, kidney disease and some cancers. An ESR may also be used to monitor an existing inflammatory condition over time.

Normal ESR values differ by age and sex. Pregnancy, the menstrual cycle, obesity, regular alcohol use, exercise and some medicines can also affect the result, so each lab's reference range should be used. Keywords: ESR, sed rate, sedimentation rate, inflammation blood test, high ESR, ESR and CRP.
