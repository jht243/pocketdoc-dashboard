Homocysteine is an amino acid made during normal protein metabolism. Vitamins B12, B6 and folate (folic acid, B9) are needed to break it down, so a shortage of any of them can raise blood levels.

Levels are reported in micromol/L (µmol/L). Laboratory reference ranges vary, but a total homocysteine above about 15 µmol/L is commonly used to define hyperhomocysteinemia. Reviews describe bands of roughly 15 to 30 µmol/L (mild), 30 to 100 µmol/L (moderate or intermediate), and above 100 µmol/L (severe); labels and exact edges differ between sources. Severe elevations point toward rare inherited disorders such as homocystinuria.

Common causes of a raised level:

- Low vitamin B12, folate or B6
- Reduced kidney function
- Genetic variants in enzymes that process homocysteine, most often MTHFR C677T
- Older age, male sex, after menopause, and smoking

MedlinePlus notes that the test is most often used to look for B12 or folate deficiency or to help diagnose homocystinuria. Routine screening of everyone for heart risk is not recommended, because the role of homocysteine in heart disease remains uncertain.
