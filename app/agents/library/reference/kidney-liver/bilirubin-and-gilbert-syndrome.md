Bilirubin (total bilirubin, T bili) comes from the breakdown of old red blood cells. The 2017 ACG Clinical Guideline on abnormal liver chemistries notes total bilirubin is usually below 1.1 mg/dL and recommends that a high total bilirubin be split (fractionated) into direct (conjugated) and indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin.

- Raised direct bilirubin generally signals liver cell disease or bile duct blockage and needs evaluation, starting with liver tests and an ultrasound.
- Raised indirect bilirubin comes from overproduction (such as hemolysis), reduced liver uptake, or reduced conjugation.

Gilbert syndrome is the most common cause of raised indirect bilirubin, affecting 3% to 7% of the US population. It is an inherited reduction in the liver enzyme UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (the UGT1A1 gene). In Gilbert syndrome total bilirubin almost never exceeds 6 mg/dL and is usually below 3 mg/dL. Fasting or illness can raise indirect bilirubin two- to threefold, and it falls again with eating.

For a healthy person with mild indirect hyperbilirubinemia below 4 mg/dL, ACG advises excluding medications that raise bilirubin and signs of hemolysis, and confirming normal ALT, AST and alkaline phosphatase. If those are normal, a presumptive diagnosis of Gilbert syndrome can be made without further routine testing. UGT1A1 genetic testing is an option if the rise stays unexplained.
