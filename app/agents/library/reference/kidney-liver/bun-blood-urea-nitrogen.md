BUN (blood urea nitrogen, urea) appears on basic and comprehensive metabolic panels. MedlinePlus, from the US National Library of Medicine, explains that it measures urea nitrogen, a waste product made when the body breaks down protein, which the kidneys clear into urine. Labs often report it next to creatinine, sometimes as a BUN-to-creatinine ratio.

BUN is affected by much more than kidney function. MedlinePlus lists:

- Higher-than-normal BUN: generally a sign the kidneys are not working well, but it can also come from dehydration, burns, certain medicines, a high-protein diet, or a recent heart attack.
- Lower-than-normal BUN: a low-protein diet, malnutrition, and liver disease.

Normal ranges vary by lab and by sex, and BUN usually rises with age. Because diet and hydration move BUN so much, a mildly high BUN with a normal creatinine and eGFR often reflects protein intake or fluid status rather than kidney damage. Kidney function itself is staged with eGFR and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio under the KDIGO 2024 CKD guideline, not with BUN. A BUN that rises together with creatinine, or a BUN outside the lab range that persists, merits clinician review.
