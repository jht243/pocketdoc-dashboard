Alkaline phosphatase (ALP, alk phos) appears on the routine comprehensive metabolic panel. The 2017 ACG Clinical Guideline on abnormal liver chemistries explains that it is not liver-specific: it is found in liver cells and also in bone, placenta, intestine and kidney, with bone the most common non-liver source.

Normal reasons ALP runs higher, per ACG:

- Children and adolescents, from bone growth.
- Older adults, especially women over 50, partly from bone turnover.
- Pregnancy, from placental ALP.
- In people with blood type O or B, after a fatty meal, from intestinal ALP.

How the source is sorted out: if ALT, AST or bilirubin are also abnormal, a liver origin is assumed. With an isolated high ALP, ACG recommends confirming with GGT or ALP isoenzyme fractionation. A normal GGT points to a non-liver source such as bone; a high GGT suggests liver or bile ducts.

When ALP is confirmed to come from the liver, ACG recommends a liver ultrasound, then tests such as antimitochondrial antibody for primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), and imaging for primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) when appropriate. Medications and infiltrative conditions can also raise ALP. A mildly raised ALP of 1 to 2 times the upper limit with a negative evaluation may be observed, per the ACG algorithm.
