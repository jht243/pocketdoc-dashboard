GGT (gamma-glutamyl transferase, gamma-GT, GGTP) is a liver enzyme found on the bile-duct side of liver cells. The 2017 ACG Clinical Guideline on abnormal liver chemistries gives it a specific, limited job.

Main use: confirming where an alkaline phosphatase (ALP) comes from. ALP is made in liver and also in bone, placenta, intestine and kidney. A raised GGT alongside a raised ALP suggests the ALP is from the liver; a normal GGT points to a non-liver source such as bone. ACG recommends confirming an ALP elevation with GGT (strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).

GGT is not specific. ACG notes it can be raised in more than 50% of people who drink heavily even without obvious liver disease, and also with pancreatic disease, heart attack, kidney failure, emphysema, diabetes, and medications such as phenytoin and barbiturates. GGT can be a complementary clue to heavy alcohol use, but it does not prove it.

For that reason ACG recommends that GGT not be used as a screening test for liver disease when the other liver tests (ALT, AST, ALP, bilirubin) are normal. An isolated high GGT with otherwise normal liver tests is usually reviewed in context of alcohol, medications and metabolic risk rather than as a liver diagnosis.
