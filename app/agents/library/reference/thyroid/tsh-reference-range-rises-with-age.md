The 2012 AACE/ATA hypothyroidism guideline reviews evidence that normal TSH (thyroid stimulating hormone) values drift upward with age:

- In NHANES III data, the 97.5th percentile of TSH rose by about 0.3 mIU/L for every 10 years of age after the 30s, and reached as high as 7.84 mIU/L in one group aged 80 and older.
- Among people over 80 with no evidence of autoimmune thyroid disease, about 12% had a TSH above 4.5 mIU/L.

The guideline concludes that very mild TSH elevations in older adults may not reflect subclinical hypothyroidism but may be a normal finding for age. This is one reason a slightly high TSH in an older person is often watched and rechecked rather than treated.

For people already being treated, the guideline recommends a target within the normal range of a third-generation TSH assay. If the lab gives no range, it suggests an upper limit of 4.12 mIU/L and a lower limit of 0.45 mIU/L in iodine-sufficient areas (Recommendation 17, Grade B). Some laboratories and groups propose tighter upper limits such as 2.5 mIU/L. The guideline did not adopt that, and any tighter "optimal" TSH target should be treated as Emerging.
