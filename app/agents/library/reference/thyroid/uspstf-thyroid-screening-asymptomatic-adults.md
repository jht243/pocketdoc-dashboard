The US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) 2015 recommendation statement concluded that the current evidence is insufficient to assess the balance of benefits and harms of screening for thyroid dysfunction in non-pregnant adults who have no symptoms (an "I statement"). It is neither a recommendation for nor against routine TSH screening.

The Task Force's reasoning:

- Trials of treating screen-detected, mostly subclinical thyroid disease for one to two years did not show improvements in quality of life or thinking.
- It could not determine whether screening reduces cardiovascular disease or death.
- Likely harms include false-positive results, the psychological effect of a diagnosis label, and overdiagnosis and overtreatment of mildly abnormal TSH values.

The USPSTF page notes that a 2024 literature scan found no new evidence to justify an updated review, so the 2015 statement stands.

This statement covers screening of people without symptoms only. It does not apply to people with symptoms of thyroid disease, pregnant people, or those with known thyroid conditions, where testing TSH is standard. Other groups, such as the 2012 AACE/ATA guideline, support case-finding in higher-risk people.
