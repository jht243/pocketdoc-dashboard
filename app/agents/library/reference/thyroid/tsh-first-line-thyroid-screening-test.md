title: TSH is the first-line blood test for thyroid function
source: AACE/ATA 2012 Clinical Practice Guidelines for Hypothyroidism in Adults

The 2012 AACE/ATA hypothyroidism guideline states that a serum TSH (thyroid stimulating hormone, thyrotropin) is the single best screening test for primary thyroid problems in most outpatient settings. TSH is made by the pituitary gland: when the thyroid is underactive (hypothyroidism), TSH usually rises; when the thyroid is overactive (hyperthyroidism), TSH usually falls.

The guideline notes two settings where TSH alone is not enough:

- Hospitalized or acutely ill patients, where TSH can shift for reasons unrelated to thyroid disease.
- Suspected central (pituitary or hypothalamic) hypothyroidism, where TSH can be low or normal even though thyroid hormone is low. Free T4 is needed in that case.

TSH results are read against the reporting laboratory's reference range, typically in mIU/L (milli-international units per liter). A TSH outside the range is usually followed by a free T4 (free thyroxine) test to classify the problem, rather than acted on alone. The guideline also says a TSH value is only interpretable as subclinical disease when thyroid function has been stable for weeks and there is no recent severe illness, so a single abnormal TSH is commonly repeated before conclusions are drawn.
