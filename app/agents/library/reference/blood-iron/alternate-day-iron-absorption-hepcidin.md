Hepcidin, a hormone made by the liver, controls how much iron the gut absorbs. An iron dose raises hepcidin, which can lower how much iron is absorbed from the next dose if it is given soon afterward. This is the idea behind alternate-day iron (every-other-day iron supplements).

The key study, two randomized trials published in Lancet Haematology in 2017, used labelled iron in iron-depleted young women with ferritin of 25 ng/mL or less:

- During the first 14 days, serum hepcidin was higher with consecutive-day dosing than with alternate-day dosing.
- Over the study period, cumulative fractional absorption was 21.8% with alternate-day dosing versus 16.3% with consecutive-day dosing, and total absorbed iron was also higher.
- In the second trial, splitting a day's iron into two doses raised hepcidin and did not improve absorption compared with a single morning dose.

This is Emerging evidence: the trials were small (40 women in the alternate-day study), short, measured absorption rather than clinical outcomes, and the authors said results needed confirming in people with iron deficiency anemia. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements notes that high amounts of supplemental iron can cause gastrointestinal side effects, and some forms are more likely to do so. The schedule, form and amount of any iron supplement are set by the prescriber or clinician, and response is checked with follow-up hemoglobin and ferritin.
