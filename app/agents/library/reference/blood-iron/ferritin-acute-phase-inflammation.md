Ferritin (iron stores) is an acute-phase reactant: the body raises it during inflammation, infection, liver disease, cancer and chronic kidney disease, independent of how much iron is actually stored. A normal or high ferritin can therefore hide iron deficiency in someone with an inflammatory condition.

The American Family Physician review on microcytosis gives these interpretive ranges:

- Ferritin below 15 ng/mL makes iron deficiency likely in an otherwise healthy person.
- Below 50 ng/mL makes iron deficiency likely in a person with a source of chronic inflammation.
- Above 100 ng/mL usually excludes iron deficiency.

The 2020 AGA guideline likewise notes ferritin is less accurate in chronic inflammatory conditions and chronic kidney disease, and suggests adding serum iron, transferrin saturation, soluble transferrin receptor or C-reactive protein (hs-CRP, CRP in mg/L) when inflammation is present. A ferritin that looks high alongside an elevated CRP is often an inflammation signal rather than iron overload; iron overload is assessed with transferrin saturation.
