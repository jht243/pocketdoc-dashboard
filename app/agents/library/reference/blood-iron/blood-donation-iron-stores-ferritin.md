Each whole blood donation removes iron along with red cells, so regular donors (blood donation, giving blood, plasma and red cell donors) are a recognized iron deficiency risk group.

FDA rules (21 CFR 630.10 and 630.15) set the minimums that a donor must pass at each visit:

- Hemoglobin of at least 12.5 g/dL or hematocrit of at least 38% for women; at least 13.0 g/dL or 39% for men.
- Whole blood no more than once every 8 weeks, and double red cell apheresis no more than once every 16 weeks.

Passing the hemoglobin check does not mean iron stores are full. In the NHLBI REDS-II Donor Iron Status Evaluation (RISE) study, absent iron stores, defined as ferritin below 12 ng/mL, were found at the final visit in 27% of female and 18% of male frequent donors, and iron-deficient red cell production in 62% and 47%. Higher donation frequency, a shorter gap between donations, female sex and younger age predicted iron depletion. The authors concluded that longer intervals between donations would reduce iron depletion.

In a 2015 JAMA randomized trial, donors who did not take iron after donating took far longer to restore hemoglobin, and 67% had not recovered their iron stores by 168 days. Whether and how much iron to take is a decision for the donor's clinician; ferritin testing shows iron stores more directly than hemoglobin.
