Thiopurines are azathioprine (Imuran), mercaptopurine (6-MP, Purixan) and thioguanine. They are used for inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis), autoimmune hepatitis, lupus, organ transplant and childhood leukemia. Two enzymes, TPMT and NUDT15, help inactivate them. People with low TPMT or NUDT15 activity build up active metabolites and are at high risk of severe bone marrow suppression (myelosuppression: very low white cells, red cells and platelets).

The CPIC thiopurine guideline (2018 update, Relling et al.) recommends:
- TPMT or NUDT15 poor metabolizers: for nonmalignant conditions such as IBD, consider a non-thiopurine immunosuppressant. For cancer treatment, start with drastically reduced doses given less often than daily, adjusted to blood counts.
- Intermediate metabolizers: consider a reduced starting dose, particularly when the usual starting dose is high, then adjust to blood counts.
- Genotype guides the starting dose; ongoing dosing relies on blood count monitoring.

NUDT15 risk variants are most common in people of East Asian ancestry and in Hispanic people with Native American ancestry, while TPMT variants are found across populations. Many clinicians check TPMT activity or genotype before a thiopurine is started. CPIC published a further update in 2025 that adds a combined TPMT and NUDT15 intermediate phenotype. Dosing decisions belong to the prescriber.
