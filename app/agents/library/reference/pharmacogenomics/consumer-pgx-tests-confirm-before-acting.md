title: Consumer pharmacogenetic test results need clinical confirmation
source: FDA Safety Communication on genetic tests with unapproved claims to predict medication response, 2018; FDA Table of Pharmacogenetic Associations, updated 2026
evidence: Strong
approved_by: pending
as_of: 2026-09
url: https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/precision-medicine/table-pharmacogenetic-associations
---

Direct-to-consumer (DTC) genetic tests such as 23andMe, and some commercial panels, report pharmacogenetic (PGx) variants that may affect how medicines are processed.

In October 2018 the FDA authorized 23andMe's Pharmacogenetic Reports test for consumers, with conditions: consumers should not use it to make treatment decisions on their own, medical decisions should be made only after discussing results with a licensed health care provider and confirming them with clinical pharmacogenetic testing, and the report must warn that its results are not a basis for medication changes made without a clinician. A day later the FDA warned that many other genetic tests claiming to predict response to specific medicines had not been reviewed and may lack supporting evidence, and that medication changes based on them could cause serious harm.

The FDA keeps a Table of Pharmacogenetic Associations (updated September 2026) in three sections: associations with therapeutic management recommendations (for example abacavir with HLA-B*57:01, and warfarin with CYP2C9 and VKORC1), associations with a potential effect on safety or response (for example simvastatin with SLCO1B1), and associations affecting drug levels only. FDA notes that listing does not mean it recommends testing before prescribing, and that patients should not change medicines without talking to their prescriber.

Consumer arrays can also miss variants, especially for complex genes like CYP2D6. A consumer PGx result is a reason for a conversation with the prescriber and possibly a clinical-grade confirmatory test, not a reason to change a medicine.
