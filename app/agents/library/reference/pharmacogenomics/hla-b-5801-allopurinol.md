Allopurinol (Zyloprim) is the most widely used medicine for lowering uric acid in gout and hyperuricemia. It is also one of the most common causes of severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCAR): Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis (SJS/TEN) and DRESS (drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms), which can be fatal.

The HLA-B*58:01 immune-gene variant is strongly linked to these reactions. CPIC grades HLA-B with allopurinol as a level A gene-drug pair. The CPIC guideline (2013, reaffirmed unchanged in its 2015 update) says allopurinol is contraindicated in HLA-B*58:01 carriers (strong recommendation) and that an alternative medicine should be considered; non-carriers can use standard dosing. The variant predicts only these severe skin reactions, not milder rashes or how well the drug works, and a negative result does not rule out every reaction. CPIC describes the association as strongest in Han Chinese and Thai populations, more modest in Korean and Japanese people, and notes that reduced kidney function is the most important non-genetic risk factor.

The 2020 American College of Rheumatology gout guideline conditionally recommends HLA-B*58:01 testing before starting allopurinol in people of Southeast Asian descent (for example Han Chinese, Korean or Thai) and in African American patients, and conditionally recommends against universal testing in other groups.

A positive HLA-B*58:01 result is worth recording permanently and sharing with every prescriber. Any choice of gout medicine belongs to the prescriber.
