Malignant hyperthermia (MH) is a rare, life-threatening reaction during general anesthesia. It is triggered by inhaled (volatile) anesthetic gases such as sevoflurane, desflurane and isoflurane, and by the muscle relaxant succinylcholine. Signs include a fast heart rate, rising carbon dioxide, muscle rigidity, high potassium, acidosis and a rapidly climbing body temperature; untreated, it can cause cardiac arrest.

MH susceptibility is inherited in an autosomal dominant way, so one copy of a causative variant is enough, and carriers usually have no symptoms until exposed. The 2018 CPIC guideline cites an estimated prevalence of the susceptibility trait of 1 in 2,000 to 1 in 3,000 people, with MH episodes occurring in 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 250,000 anesthetics. RYR1 accounts for about 70% of susceptible people and CACNA1S about 1%. CPIC grades both genes with these anesthetics as level A.

CPIC recommendations for a carrier of one of the 50 recognized causative variants (strong): volatile anesthetics and succinylcholine are relatively contraindicated and used only in extraordinary circumstances. Safe options exist, including regional or local anesthesia, total intravenous anesthesia and non-depolarizing muscle relaxants, with the anesthesia machine prepared to remove trigger agents.

A negative genetic result does not rule out susceptibility, because the cause is unknown in about half of confirmed cases; a personal or family history of an anesthesia reaction still matters. Blood relatives of a carrier should be evaluated. Any MH-related result should be shared with the surgeon and anesthesiologist before every procedure, including dental sedation.
