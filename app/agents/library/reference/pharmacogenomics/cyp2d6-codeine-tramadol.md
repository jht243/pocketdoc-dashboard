Codeine (in Tylenol with codeine and some cough syrups) and tramadol (Ultram) are opioid pain medicines that the liver enzyme CYP2D6 converts into more potent forms: codeine to morphine, and tramadol to its active metabolite.

The 2021 CPIC opioid guideline (Crews et al.) recommends:
- CYP2D6 ultrarapid metabolizers: avoid codeine and avoid tramadol because of the potential for serious toxicity. They convert too much too fast, which can cause dangerous sedation and slowed breathing. Strong recommendation.
- CYP2D6 poor metabolizers: avoid codeine and avoid tramadol because of the possibility of diminished pain relief. Strong recommendation.
- Normal metabolizers: standard label-based use.
- Intermediate metabolizers: standard label-based use; if pain relief is inadequate, a different opioid may be considered.
- When codeine is avoided, CPIC points to an opioid other than tramadol, and the reverse, since both depend on CYP2D6.

CPIC judged the evidence for CYP2D6 with hydrocodone, oxycodone and methadone, and for the OPRM1 and COMT genes, too limited or weak for clinical recommendations. The FDA separately restricts codeine and tramadol in children, partly because of ultrarapid metabolism.

A CYP2D6 result is worth adding to the medication record and sharing with any prescriber, dentist or surgeon before pain medicine is chosen.
