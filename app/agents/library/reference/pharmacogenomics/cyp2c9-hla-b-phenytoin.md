Phenytoin (Dilantin) and its injectable precursor fosphenytoin (Cerebyx) are antiseizure (antiepileptic) medicines with a narrow safe range, so small changes in blood level matter. Two genes affect them, and CPIC grades both CYP2C9 and HLA-B with phenytoin and fosphenytoin as level A pairs.

HLA-B*15:02: carriers have a higher risk of Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis (SJS/TEN), severe skin reactions. The 2020 CPIC update (Karnes et al.) strongly recommends that phenytoin and fosphenytoin not be used in carriers who have not taken them before, and advises avoiding carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine as well; other aromatic antiseizure drugs such as lamotrigine and eslicarbazepine call for caution. Because these reactions usually appear within the first three months, someone who has taken phenytoin continuously for longer than three months without a skin reaction may cautiously use it again (optional recommendation). The same allele's link to carbamazepine is covered in the HLA-B abacavir and carbamazepine passage.

CYP2C9: this liver enzyme clears phenytoin. Intermediate metabolizers with an activity score of 1 and poor metabolizers build up higher levels and more side effects such as unsteadiness, dizziness and confusion. CPIC recommends a usual first dose followed by a lower maintenance dose guided by blood levels, a moderate recommendation for intermediate and a strong one for poor metabolizers.

Phenytoin blood-level monitoring stays essential regardless of genotype. Dosing belongs to the neurologist or prescriber.
