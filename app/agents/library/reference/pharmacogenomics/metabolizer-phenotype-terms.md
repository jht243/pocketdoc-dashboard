Pharmacogenomic (PGx) reports describe each gene result as a predicted phenotype. CPIC published standard terms in 2017 so that labs and guidelines use the same words.

For drug-metabolizing enzymes such as CYP2D6, CYP2C19, CYP2C9, TPMT, NUDT15 and DPYD:
- Ultrarapid metabolizer: much higher enzyme activity than usual.
- Rapid metabolizer: somewhat higher activity than normal (used for CYP2C19).
- Normal metabolizer: fully functional enzyme. This term replaced the older "extensive metabolizer".
- Intermediate metabolizer: reduced activity, between normal and poor.
- Poor metabolizer: little or no enzyme activity, usually two non-functional copies of the gene.

For drug transporters such as SLCO1B1 and ABCG2, the terms are increased, normal, decreased and poor function. For HLA genes such as HLA-B*57:01 or HLA-B*15:02, the result is reported as positive or negative for the high-risk allele.

What a phenotype means depends on the drug. For a prodrug that must be activated (codeine, clopidogrel), a poor metabolizer gets too little active drug; for a drug the enzyme inactivates (citalopram, many PPIs), a poor metabolizer builds up higher levels. Other medicines can also block or boost an enzyme and change the effective phenotype, which is called phenoconversion. Results guide the prescriber; they are not instructions to change a medicine.
