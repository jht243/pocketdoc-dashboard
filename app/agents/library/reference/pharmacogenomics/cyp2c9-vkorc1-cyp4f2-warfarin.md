Warfarin (Coumadin, Jantoven) has a narrow safe range, and the dose that reaches a target INR of 2 to 3 varies widely between people. Genes explain part of that variation:

- CYP2C9 breaks down the more potent form of warfarin. Reduced-function alleles (CYP2C9*2 and *3, and in people of African ancestry *5, *6, *8 and *11) mean slower clearance, a lower dose requirement and higher bleeding risk early in treatment.
- VKORC1 is the enzyme warfarin blocks. The VKORC1 -1639G>A variant increases sensitivity, so carriers usually need less warfarin.
- CYP4F2*3 is linked to a modestly higher dose requirement in people of European or Asian ancestry.

The 2017 CPIC update (Johnson et al.) recommends that, for people not of African ancestry, the starting dose be calculated with a published pharmacogenetic dosing algorithm that includes VKORC1 and CYP2C9*2 and *3, and that an alternative anticoagulant may be considered for CYP2C9 poor metabolizers (strong recommendations). For people of African ancestry, CPIC gives moderate-strength adjustments based on CYP2C9*5, *6, *8, *11 and rs12777823, and advises dosing clinically if those variants were not tested. The FDA lists warfarin with CYP2C9 and VKORC1 among associations with therapeutic management recommendations.

INR monitoring remains essential whatever the genotype. A result on file is worth sharing with the anticoagulation clinic or prescriber.
