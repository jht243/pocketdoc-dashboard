G6PD (glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase) protects red blood cells from oxidative stress. In G6PD deficiency, certain medicines, infections and fava beans can trigger hemolysis, a rapid breakdown of red blood cells that causes acute hemolytic anemia, with fatigue, dark urine and yellowing of the skin or eyes. The 2022 CPIC guideline estimates almost 5% of the world's population has G6PD deficiency, more often in people from malaria-endemic regions of Africa and Asia, where some groups have rates of 30% or more.

The gene is on the X chromosome. Males have one copy, so their genotype predicts their enzyme status; females with one deficient copy can have anywhere from normal to deficient activity, so CPIC says an enzyme activity test is needed to know their status.

CPIC classifies medicines by risk and grades G6PD as level A for rasburicase, dapsone, methylene blue, primaquine, tafenoquine and nitrofurantoin:
- High risk, to be avoided in deficiency: rasburicase and pegloticase (gout and tumor-lysis drugs), dapsone, methylene blue, tafenoquine, toluidine blue, and primaquine at standard malaria doses.
- Medium risk, used with caution and monitoring: nitrofurantoin (a urinary tract infection antibiotic).
- Low to no risk based on G6PD status: low-dose aspirin, sulfamethoxazole and other sulfonamides, ciprofloxacin and other fluoroquinolones, chloroquine, hydroxychloroquine, vitamin C and vitamin K.

CPIC notes that many drug labels carry G6PD warnings the evidence does not support. A known G6PD deficiency belongs on the medication record and should be mentioned before any new prescription.
