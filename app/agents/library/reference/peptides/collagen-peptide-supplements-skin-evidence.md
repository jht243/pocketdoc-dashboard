Collagen peptides (hydrolyzed collagen, collagen powder, marine collagen) are among the most popular "peptide" products. They are sold as foods or dietary supplements, not drugs, so FDA does not review them for effectiveness before sale. They are different from injectable research peptides such as BPC-157.

The best-known claim is less wrinkling and better skin hydration and elasticity. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis in the American Journal of Medicine pooled 23 randomized controlled trials with 1,474 participants:

- Pooling all 23 trials, collagen appeared to improve skin hydration, elasticity and wrinkles.
- But trials without pharmaceutical or supplement-company funding showed no effect on any of these, while industry-funded trials did.
- High-quality trials showed no significant effect in any category; only lower-quality trials showed improvement in elasticity.
- The authors concluded there is currently no clinical evidence to support collagen supplements to prevent or treat skin aging.

Industry groups disputed the analysis, and later reviews have been more mixed, so the question is not settled. Claims for collagen and skin, hair, nails or joints should be described as Emerging. Product quality varies; third-party testing seals are covered in a separate supplements passage.
