"Peptide" simply means a short chain of amino acids. Some peptide medicines are FDA-approved; many products marketed as peptides are not.

FDA-approved peptide drugs have passed FDA review for safety, effectiveness and manufacturing quality for specific uses. Examples:

- GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) and the GIP/GLP-1 agonist tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound), approved for type 2 diabetes and, for certain products, chronic weight management.
- Tesamorelin (Egrifta), a growth hormone-releasing factor analog approved in 2010 to reduce excess abdominal fat in adults with HIV-associated lipodystrophy. Its labeling states it is not indicated for weight loss.
- Insulin and many others.

Unapproved peptides, including BPC-157, TB-500, ipamorelin, CJC-1295, MOTS-c and Epitalon, have no FDA-approved product. They reach people through compounding pharmacies, which FDA says are not reviewed for safety or effectiveness before marketing, or through "research use only" websites.

The distinction matters for quality, dosing accuracy and recourse when something goes wrong. Agents explain the difference; whether any peptide is appropriate is decided by a licensed clinician.
