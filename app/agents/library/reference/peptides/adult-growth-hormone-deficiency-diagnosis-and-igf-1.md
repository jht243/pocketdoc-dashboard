IGF-1 (insulin-like growth factor 1, somatomedin C) is made mainly by the liver in response to growth hormone (GH), so it is used as a blood marker of growth hormone activity. Labs report it against age-adjusted reference ranges, often as an SDS or z-score, because IGF-1 falls naturally with age.

The 2019 American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists (AACE) adult growth hormone deficiency guideline explains its limits:

- Random GH and IGF-1 levels cannot be used alone to diagnose adult GH deficiency. GH is released in pulses, and IGF-1 can be lowered by poor nutrition, poorly controlled diabetes, chronic illness, kidney failure and chronic liver disease. Oral estrogen also affects it.
- Adult GH deficiency is considered mainly in people with a history of pituitary or hypothalamic disease, such as a pituitary tumor, surgery or radiation, or childhood-onset deficiency. The guideline distinguishes this from the normal decline of GH with aging.
- Diagnosis is usually confirmed with a GH stimulation test. The insulin tolerance test remains the gold standard but is used less often for safety reasons; the glucagon stimulation test and the macimorelin oral test, FDA-approved for this purpose in December 2017, are alternatives.
- Stimulation testing can be skipped in some people, such as those with three or more other pituitary hormone deficiencies plus a low IGF-1.

Labelled Moderate because several recommendations rest on expert consensus. A low or "suboptimal" IGF-1 on a wellness panel does not by itself establish GH deficiency.
